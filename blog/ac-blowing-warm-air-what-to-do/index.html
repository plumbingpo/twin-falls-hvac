First Steps When Your AC Stops Cooling in Twin Falls, ID

Home / Blog / First Steps When Your AC Stops Cooling in Twin Falls, ID

It’s a hot afternoon here in Twin Falls, and the last thing you want is for your air conditioner to start blowing warm air. That familiar rush of cool, refreshing air is essential for comfort when temperatures climb. But what happens when that cool breeze turns into a lukewarm sigh? Don't panic! While a professional HVAC technician from Twin Falls HVAC is always ready to help, there are several common issues you can perform yourself before picking up the phone. Frequently, the problem might be something relatively minor that you can resolve without a service call, saving you time and discomfort. Let's walk through some of the common culprits when your AC starts acting up.

1. Check Your Thermostat Settings

This might seem obvious, but it's surprisingly common. Before you assume the worst, take a moment to double-check your thermostat settings. Is it set to "Cool"? Sometimes, especially after a change in seasons or if someone else has adjusted it, the thermostat can get switched to "Heat" or "Fan Only." If it's on "Fan Only," the fan will run, but the compressor, which is responsible for cooling the air, won't engage. Make sure the temperature setting is also lower than the current room temperature. If you have a programmable thermostat, confirm that the schedule isn't overriding your desired setting. A simple adjustment here could be all it takes to get that cool air flowing again. If you're using a smart thermostat, check its app for any error messages or unusual settings.

2. Inspect Your Air Filter

A dirty air filter is one of the most frequent causes of HVAC problems, including your AC blowing warm air. The air filter's job is to trap dust, pollen, pet dander, and other airborne particles, preventing them from entering your HVAC system and circulating throughout your home. When the filter becomes heavily soiled, it restricts airflow. This means less air can pass over the evaporator coil, which is where the cooling process happens. Consequently, the coil can get too cold and even freeze over. When an evaporator coil freezes, your AC essentially stops being able to cool the air effectively, leading to warm air being circulated. Go to your indoor unit (usually in a closet, attic, or basement) and pull out the air filter. If it looks dirty, replace it immediately with a clean one. It's a good practice to inspect your filter once a month and change it every 30 to 90 days, depending on factors like pets, allergies, and household activity. A clean filter not only helps your AC cool better but also improves indoor air quality and the overall efficiency of your system, saving you money on energy bills.

3. Check for Blocked Condensate Drain Line

Your air conditioning system removes humidity from your home's air during the cooling process. This moisture condenses on the evaporator coil and drains away through a condensate line, often leading outside. Over time, algae, mold, and sludge can build up in this line, causing a blockage. When the condensate line is blocked, the water backs up, often triggering a safety switch that shuts down the outdoor unit (the condenser) to prevent overflowing. If the condenser isn't running, your system won't be able to cool. Look for water stains near your indoor unit or an indicator light on your thermostat. Sometimes, you can clear a minor blockage yourself. Locate the condensate drain line (it's usually a PVC pipe exiting the indoor unit). If you can access it, try to clear it using a shop vac at the outdoor discharge point. However, be cautious and avoid using harsh chemicals. If you're unsure or the blockage is stubborn, it's best to call a professional from Twin Falls HVAC.

4. Examine the Outdoor Unit (Condenser)

The outdoor unit, or condenser, is where your AC releases the heat it has absorbed from your home. For this process to work efficiently, the condenser coil needs to be clean and have unobstructed airflow. Over time, leaves, grass clippings, dirt, and other debris can accumulate on the coil fins, acting as an insulating blanket. This restricts the system's ability to dissipate heat, leading to reduced cooling capacity and potentially warm air. Carefully turn off the power to your outdoor unit at the disconnect switch (usually a small box near the unit) and at your main electrical panel. Then, gently hose off the condenser coil fins. Make sure to spray at an angle that won't damage the delicate fins. Also, check for any vegetation, bushes, or other objects that are too close to the unit, as these can also impede airflow. A clean condenser allows your system to breathe and cool much more effectively.

5. Check Circuit Breakers

Your HVAC system relies on electricity to power both the indoor and outdoor units. If your AC is blowing warm air, it's possible that a circuit breaker has tripped, cutting power to either the indoor fan or the outdoor compressor. Go to your home's main electrical panel (usually in your basement, garage, or utility closet). Look for any tripped breakers – these will typically be in the "off" position or halfway between "on" and "off." If you find a tripped breaker labeled "AC" or "Furnace," flip it completely to the "off" position, then firmly back to "on". Be aware that if a breaker trips repeatedly, it indicates a more serious electrical issue that requires professional attention. Never force a breaker that resists resetting. While you're at it, also check the disconnect switch near your outdoor unit. It should be in the "on" position. If it was off, simply flip it back on.

6. Consider Refrigerant Levels (Low Refrigerant)

If you've gone through all the above steps and your AC is still blowing warm air, the problem might be low refrigerant levels. Refrigerant (often called Freon, though that's a brand name) is the substance that absorbs heat from your home's air and releases it outside. Unlike fuel in a car, refrigerant is not "consumed" by your AC system. If your refrigerant levels are low, it almost always indicates a leak somewhere in the sealed system. A system with low refrigerant cannot effectively cool your home. Symptoms of low refrigerant include warm air, ice on the evaporator coil (even after changing the filter), and a hissing or bubbling sound coming from the unit. Addressing a refrigerant leak is a complex task that requires specialized tools and knowledge. It involves identifying the leak, repairing it, and then recharging the system with the correct amount and type of refrigerant. This is not a DIY job and should always be handled by a professional from Twin Falls HVAC to ensure safety and proper system operation. Improper refrigerant handling can be dangerous and is regulated by environmental agencies.
